**Short answer:** South America is too big for one best month, but if you want a single answer for a multi-country trip, it is **September**. It is the only month in which three of the five South American weather stations on our sheets pass the plain rule our month lists use: the Cusco highlands in [Peru](/peru/#weather), [Brasília](/brazil/#weather) and [Santiago](/chile/#weather). After that the answer depends on where you are going. The Cusco station passes in **nine months, March to November**. Brasília passes **May to September**. Santiago passes in **seven months**, February to April and September to December. [Bogotá](/colombia/#weather) passes only in **January and February**, and [Buenos Aires](/argentina/#weather) **never** does.

That is an editorial call, made from five tables. Here is exactly how we made it, and what the tables cannot tell you.

## The rule, and the five stations

Our [month lists](/best-places-to-visit-in-september/) use one rule: a place passes in a month when its **average daily high is from 20 °C to 30 °C** and it gets **less than 100 mm** of precipitation. Nothing else counts. We have no sourced data on crowds, prices or festivals, so we left them out.

We have five South American country sheets, and each one prints a single named station with its 1991–2020 monthly normals, distributed by NOAA NCEI:

| Sheet | Station | Height | What it is |
|---|---|---:|---|
| [Colombia](/colombia/) | El Dorado CATAM (AUT), Bogotá | 2,547 m | The capital, on the high plateau. Not the Caribbean coast. |
| [Peru](/peru/) | Granja Kcayra | 3,214 m | Highland Cusco region. Not Lima, which is not in the file set. |
| [Brazil](/brazil/) | Brasília | 1,161 m | The capital, inland. Not Rio de Janeiro, which is not in the file set. |
| [Argentina](/argentina/) | Buenos Aires Observatorio | 25 m | The capital. Not Patagonia. |
| [Chile](/chile/) | Quinta Normal, Santiago | 520 m | The capital, in the central valley. |

Three of the five are high up, and that matters more than latitude. Bogotá's station sits at 4°42′ N, by its NCEI file, close to the equator, yet at 2,547 m its average high stays between 18.54 °C and 20.18 °C all year. A table for a coast, a jungle or a glacier in the same country would look nothing like these.

No month is empty and no month passes everywhere. That is the honest shape of a continent that runs from the Caribbean to Cape Horn: there is always somewhere that works, and never one month that works for all of it.

## Peru's highlands: April to October is the dry season, and the table agrees

Granja Kcayra is the most forgiving station of the five. Its average high stays between 20.2 °C and 22.2 °C all year, so it never fails on temperature. It fails only on rain, and only from December to February: 114.9 mm in December, 145.8 mm in January (the wettest month) and 126.4 mm in February. March squeaks through at 97.3 mm.

Then it dries out almost completely. May has 6.3 mm, June 3.7 mm (the driest month), July 4.2 mm and August 5.2 mm.

PROMPERÚ's official site agrees with the table. Its climate page, read on 22 November 2026, says the Andes have two seasons: April to October, "with sunny days, cold nights, and little rain (an ideal time to visit)", and November to March, with abundant rain. The page calls the dry months "summer" and the wet months "winter", which is the reverse of the calendar seasons south of the equator, so go by the months, not the labels. It says the Amazon has the same split: a rainy season from November to March and a low-rainfall season from April to October that it calls ideal for visiting.

The catch is the nights. The same dry months are the coldest at Granja Kcayra: the average low is -0.8 °C in June and -1.3 °C in July. Those are averages, so many nights go lower. Pack for frost.

PROMPERÚ says the coast runs on a different clock, with heavy rains from December to April, "especially in the higher areas". We have no Lima table, so the [Peru sheet](/peru/) does not rate the coast.

## Brasília: May to September, then the rains come back hard

[Brazil's](/brazil/) station is a textbook wet-and-dry table. From May to September it passes every month, with highs from 25.3 °C to 29.1 °C and almost no rain: 26.9 mm in May, 3.3 mm in June, 1.5 mm in July (the driest month), 16.3 mm in August and 38.1 mm in September.

October breaks it. Rain jumps to 141.8 mm, and from November to March every month is over 175 mm, with November the wettest at 253.1 mm. Temperature is never the problem here; every month's average high is between 25.3 °C and 29.1 °C.

Brasília is one inland city in a very large country, and the sheet says Rio de Janeiro is not in the file set. We read no official Brazilian season page for this story, so we are not extending the Brasília table to the coast or the Amazon.

## Santiago: spring and autumn, not high summer or winter

[Chile's](/chile/) Quinta Normal station barely gets rain in summer. January has 0.6 mm, February 1.3 mm, December 3.0 mm. Its problem is temperature at both ends:

- **High summer is a touch too hot.** January averages 30.4 °C, just over the 30 °C line. February (29.7 °C) and December (29.0 °C) stay under it and pass.
- **Winter is too cool.** From May to August the average high is 15.7 °C to 19.2 °C. June is the wettest month, at 80.4 mm.

So Santiago's good months are **February to April** and **September to December**. September (20.1 °C, 25.3 mm) is the first month back over the line.

SERNATUR's Chile Travel climate page, read on 22 November 2026, describes the central valley in the same terms: a Mediterranean climate with warm, dry summers from December to March and cool, rainy winters from June to August. It also says the south of Chile is temperate and rainy, with a high probability of rain throughout the year, even in summer. Santiago's table is the capital, not Patagonia or the Atacama.

## Bogotá: never warm, briefly dry

[Colombia's](/colombia/) station is the odd one out. Its average high moves less than two degrees across the whole year, from 18.54 °C in July to 20.18 °C in February. Only January (20.14 °C) and February (20.18 °C) reach 20 °C; December misses at 19.77 °C.

January is also the driest month, at 32.89 mm, and February has 51.38 mm, so those are Bogotá's two passing months. April is the wettest, at 116.71 mm, and May, October and November also top 100 mm.

Bogotá failing the rule most of the year is about altitude, not about a bad climate. A daytime high around 19 °C is cool, not cold. Bring a layer and you can go in any month; just know that the warm-and-dry window is short.

## Buenos Aires: close, but never quite

[Argentina's](/argentina/) Buenos Aires Observatorio station does not pass the rule in any month, and it misses in two different ways.

- **Summer is warm but wet.** From December to April the highs are pleasant, from 23.2 °C to 30.1 °C, but every month has 120 mm or more. January is the wettest at 134.4 mm.
- **Winter is dry enough but cool.** June has the least rain, 61.5 mm, but its average high is 16.4 °C.

The near-misses are the shoulder months. May averages 19.4 °C with 93.5 mm, and September 19.7 °C with 80.6 mm. Both are dry enough and less than a degree short. If you want Buenos Aires, our editorial pick is late September or May, and pack a jacket either way. The station is the capital; the Argentina sheet says Patagonia is not this table.

## Our picks

- **One month for a multi-country trip:** September (Cusco region, Brasília and Santiago all pass).
- **Peru's highlands:** May to August for the driest months, with frosty nights; the official dry season is April to October.
- **Brasília:** June to August, the three driest months.
- **Santiago:** September to November or February to April.
- **Bogotá:** January or February.
- **Buenos Aires:** no month passes; May and September are the closest.
- **The months to avoid if you are combining Peru's highlands and Brasília:** December to February, when both stations fail on rain.

## Before you go

The plugs change at almost every border. Our sheets list Types A and B at 110 V in [Colombia](/colombia/#plugs); A, B and C at 220 V in [Peru](/peru/#plugs); C and N at 127 V and 220 V in [Brazil](/brazil/#plugs); Type I at 220 V in [Argentina](/argentina/#plugs); and C and L at 220 V in [Chile](/chile/#plugs). Our [plug list](/plug-types-by-country/) has them side by side.

So do the emergency numbers. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office's lists, as printed on our sheets, give police 123 in Colombia, 105 in Peru, 190 or 911 in Brazil, 911 or 101 in Argentina and 133 in Chile. None of the five lists 112; our [112 story](/journal/112-emergency-number/) explains where that number does work.

Tipping is close to 10% in all five, and each sheet quotes an official line: Colombia's consumer regulator says any suggested tip is voluntary and at most 10%; PROMPERÚ says about 10%; Visit Brasil says about 10% where it is not already included; Argentina.travel says 10% is usual; and Chile's labour directorate says restaurants must suggest at least 10%, which you may decline. All five drive on the right.

The U.S. State Department's advisories differ a lot. As our sheets print them, Argentina is at Level 1, Peru, Brazil and Chile at Level 2, and Colombia at Level 3, "Reconsider Travel". Read the full advisory on each sheet before you book.

## What this does not cover

Five stations are not a continent. We have no table for the Amazon, the Atacama, Patagonia, Rio de Janeiro, Lima, the Caribbean coast of Colombia, or for Ecuador, Bolivia, Uruguay, Paraguay or the Guianas. Ecuador was left out of our sheets because no usable named-station normal for Quito or Guayaquil was found. We do not rank months on prices, crowds or events, because we have no sourced data on them. Every figure above is printed, with its station and its source, on the sheet we linked.
