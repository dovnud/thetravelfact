**Short answer:** 112 is the European emergency number. The European Commission says it is "available everywhere in the EU, free of charge", from fixed and mobile phones, and that one call reaches an ambulance, the fire brigade or the police. It also works as an all-services number in several countries outside the EU: on our sheets, **Switzerland, Iceland, Turkey and India** all list 112 for ambulance, fire and police.

The catch is that 112 is not a universal code. In **South Korea** the UK government lists 112 as the police number only, with 119 for ambulance and fire. In **Indonesia** it lists 112 for fire, with police on 110. In **Mexico, Canada, the Caribbean and most of Latin America** the number on our sheets is 911, in **Japan** it is 110 and 119, and in **Australia** it is 000.

This story uses two kinds of source. What 112 is, and the EU rules behind it, come from the European Commission's [112 page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/112), read on 16 November 2026. Every country's numbers come from the emergency line on our sheets, which quotes the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office's travel advice (GOV.UK) for that country, read on 15 November 2026. We use the FCDO because it publishes the same "Emergency services" box for every country, in the same format. Where a sheet uses a different source, we say so.

## What the EU actually promises

The Commission's page makes five practical points:

1. **One number, any service.** You can call 112 to contact "any emergency service: an ambulance, the fire brigade or the police". A trained operator either handles the call or transfers it to the right service, "depending on the national organisation of emergency services".
2. **Language.** "Operators in many countries can answer the calls not only in their national language, but also in English or French." Note the word *many*, not *all*.
3. **Location.** If you do not know where you are, the operator "will identify where the person making the call is physically located".
4. **It sits alongside national numbers.** 112 "functions alongside existing national emergency numbers". Only eight countries use 112 as their *only* national emergency number: Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal, Romania and Sweden.
5. **Your phone will remind you.** Under the Roaming Regulation, mobile operators must send an SMS about 112 to people travelling to another EU country.

The Commission adds that 112 became the single European emergency number in 1991, that new cars carry eCall, which "automatically dials 112 in the event of a serious road accident", and that 11 February is European 112 Day. It also says 112 is used in some countries outside the EU, naming Switzerland and South Africa, and "is available worldwide on GSM mobile networks". That last sentence is about mobile networks, not about who answers; it is not the same as a country's own emergency service listing 112. For that, we go country by country.

Two of those rows deserve a second look.

**Poland.** The FCDO box for Poland lists 997, 998 and 999 and does not mention 112. Poland is an EU member (it is on the EU's own list of members that have not yet adopted the euro), so the Commission's statement that 112 is available everywhere in the EU covers it. We are not adding 112 to the Poland sheet itself, because the sheet prints only what its cited page says. If you are in [Kraków](/poland/krakow/), either works by those two sources; 112 is the one you will remember.

**Germany and Italy.** These are the two countries where "112" lands on a specific service in the FCDO list. In Germany it is ambulance and fire, with police on 110. In Italy the FCDO lists 112 as police, with ambulance on 118 and fire on 115. The Commission's point that an operator transfers the call to the right service is the reason 112 is still the safe default in both.

That page does not list ambulance or fire numbers, so we do not print them, and we do not print 112 for the UK either. It is recorded in our [gaps](/sources/#gaps).

## Outside Europe: where 112 means something else

This is where a European habit can cost you time. The FCDO lists 112 in five more of our countries, and in three of them it is not an all-services line.

- **[India](/india/#emergency): 112 for ambulance, fire and police.** The plainest match with Europe.
- **[South Africa](/south-africa/#emergency): 112 for "all emergencies from mobiles"**, with 10177 for ambulance and fire and 10111 for police. The Commission also names South Africa as a 112 country.
- **[South Korea](/south-korea/#emergency): 112 is police.** Ambulance and fire are 119. In [Seoul](/south-korea/seoul/), a medical emergency is 119, not 112.
- **[Indonesia](/indonesia/#emergency): 112 is listed for fire.** Ambulance is 118 and police 110. That covers [Bali](/indonesia/bali/).
- **[Vietnam](/vietnam/#emergency): 112 is national search and rescue.** Ambulance is 115, fire 114, police 113, and the FCDO adds that emergency numbers in Vietnam are operated in Vietnamese only.

## Where to dial something else entirely

Everywhere else on our sheets, the FCDO's list does not include 112. These are the numbers it gives:

- **911 for ambulance, fire and police:** [Mexico](/mexico/#emergency), [Canada](/canada/#emergency), [the United States](/united-states/new-york/#emergency), [Hawaii](/hawaii/#emergency), [Dominican Republic](/dominican-republic/#emergency) (the FCDO adds that 911 does not have full coverage across the country) and [Costa Rica](/costa-rica/#emergency). [Puerto Rico](/puerto-rico/#emergency) is 9-1-1, from its own Department of Public Safety.
- **911 plus separate numbers:** [Panama](/panama/#emergency) lists 911 for all three, and also ambulance and fire 103, police 104. [The Bahamas](/bahamas/#emergency) lists 911 or 919. [Aruba](/aruba/#emergency) lists ambulance and fire 911 and police 100. In [Brazil](/brazil/#emergency) and [Argentina](/argentina/#emergency), 911 is listed for police only.
- **000:** [Australia](/australia/#emergency), for all three. **111:** [New Zealand](/new-zealand/#emergency), for all three.
- **110 and 119:** [Japan](/japan/#emergency): police 110, ambulance and fire 119.
- **191:** [Thailand](/thailand/#emergency), for all three, with ambulance also on 1669, fire on 199 and tourist police on 1155.
- **Three different numbers, none of them 112 or 911:** [Singapore](/singapore/#emergency) (police 999, ambulance and fire 995), [United Arab Emirates](/united-arab-emirates/dubai/#emergency) (police 999, ambulance 998, fire 997), [Morocco](/morocco/#emergency) (police 190, ambulance and fire 150), [Egypt](/egypt/#emergency) (police 122, ambulance 123, fire 180, tourist police 126), [Jamaica](/jamaica/#emergency) (police 119, ambulance and fire 110), [Colombia](/colombia/#emergency) (police and ambulance 123, fire 119), [Peru](/peru/#emergency) (police 105, ambulance 106, fire 116) and [Chile](/chile/#emergency) (police 133, ambulance 131, fire 132).

Notice the collisions. 119 is the police in Jamaica and the ambulance in Japan and South Korea. 999 is the police in Singapore and Dubai and the ambulance in Poland. 110 is the police in Japan and Indonesia and an ambulance in Jamaica. A number you memorised on one trip can be the wrong service on the next.

## Our take: save one number per trip, not one number for life

1. **In the EU, 112 is the safe default.** It is the one number the Commission guarantees everywhere in the EU, and the operator routes the call.
2. **Outside Europe, look up the destination.** 112 is all-services in India and on mobiles in South Africa, but it is the police in South Korea and fire in Indonesia. In the Americas the number on our sheets is usually 911, and in Japan, Australia, New Zealand, Thailand and Singapore it is something else again.
3. **Save the number before you land.** Every sheet on this site has an Emergency line near the bottom, with its source and the date we read it. Put it in your phone contacts under the city name.

We are not telling you how fast any service responds, whether operators speak English (except where the FCDO or the Commission says so), or whether a call works without a local SIM. None of our sources state those things country by country.

## Quick answers

**What is 112?** The European emergency number. The European Commission says it is free and available everywhere in the EU, from fixed and mobile phones, for ambulance, fire and police.

**Does 112 work in the United States?** We have no official US source that says it does. The FCDO lists 911 for the USA, and that is the number our US sheets print. Dial 911.

**Is 112 the same as 911?** In the EU and on our India, Iceland, Turkey and Switzerland sheets, it plays the same all-services role. It is not the same everywhere: it is the police in South Korea and fire in Indonesia.

**Which countries use only 112?** The Commission names eight: Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Malta, the Netherlands, Portugal, Romania and Sweden.

**What is the emergency number in the UK?** GOV.UK says 999 for the police when a crime is in progress or someone is in immediate danger, and 101 for non-emergencies.
