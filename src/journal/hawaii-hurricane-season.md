**Short answer:** Hawaii's hurricane season is the **central Pacific hurricane season**, and it runs from **1 June to 30 November**. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center names **July, August and September** as the peak months. In a near-normal year the whole central Pacific basin sees **4 or 5 tropical cyclones**, and most of them do not hit the islands. Hurricane season is also Hawaii's dry season. The rain comes in the other half of the year.

Everything below comes from four official pages, all read on 18 November 2026: the [National Weather Service Honolulu outlook for 2026](https://www.weather.gov/hfo/hurricaneOutlook2026), the [Climate Prediction Center's eastern and central Pacific outlook](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/Epac_hurr/index.html), the central Pacific section of the [National Hurricane Center's climatology page](https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/climo/), and the NWS Honolulu office's [Climate of Hawaiʻi](https://www.weather.gov/hfo/climate_summary). The temperature and rain figures are the 1991–2020 normals printed on our [Hawaii](/hawaii/), [Honolulu](/hawaii/honolulu/) and [Maui](/hawaii/maui/) sheets. Each sheet names its station. We have not added any figure that is not on one of those pages.

## The official window

The Climate Prediction Center puts both Pacific seasons in one sentence: "The eastern Pacific hurricane season officially runs from May 15th through November 30th while the central Pacific hurricane season officially runs from June 1st through November 30th." The NWS Honolulu outlook gives the same dates for the central Pacific.

The **central Pacific** is the stretch of ocean that matters for Hawaii. The NWS defines it as the area **north of the equator between 140°W and the International Date Line**, and the islands sit inside it. The NHC adds that tropical cyclones forming between 5 and 30 degrees north typically move toward the west, so the busier eastern Pacific next door is part of the picture too.

> The central Pacific hurricane season runs from 1 June to 30 November. The peak months are July, August and September.

The peak line comes from the Climate Prediction Center. It calls July to September the peak of both the eastern and the central Pacific seasons and abbreviates it JAS. If your dates fall in those three months, you are travelling through the busiest part of a typical season. Outside them you are still inside the window until 30 November.

## How many storms is normal?

Hawaii's basin is much quieter than the Atlantic's. Two NOAA figures say so:

- The NWS Honolulu outlook says **a near-normal central Pacific season has 4 or 5 tropical cyclones**. "Tropical cyclones" here include tropical depressions, tropical storms and hurricanes, not just hurricanes.
- The Climate Prediction Center gives the **1991–2020 average as 4.4 combined named storms and tropical depressions** for the central Pacific.

For comparison, the [Caribbean hurricane season story](/journal/caribbean-hurricane-season/) quotes the National Hurricane Center's Atlantic average: 14 named storms, 7 hurricanes and 3 major hurricanes a year. The Atlantic counts named storms only. The central Pacific figure also counts tropical depressions, so the gap is wider than 4.4 against 14 suggests.

The National Hurricane Center's climatology page breaks down the central Pacific record for 1971 to 2008, the satellite era. It counts **163 systems**: **58 hurricanes (36%)**, **46 tropical storms (28%)** and **59 tropical depressions (36%)**. In that record roughly one system in three reached hurricane strength. Those 163 systems are basin-wide. The page charts separately the storms that came within 200 miles and within 75 miles of Hawaii, and it warns that "storms that do not make landfall in Hawaii can still cause considerable damage, mostly from winds and surf."

## What the NWS says about Hawaii and hurricanes

The NWS Honolulu climate page puts it carefully. Hawaii "usually does not have hurricanes and hailstorms," it says, and then adds that "thunderstorms, lightning, hail, floods, hurricanes, tornadoes, and droughts are not unknown." Its conclusion is that these events "are usually less frequent and less severe than their counterparts in continental regions."

That is the right frame for a trip. A hurricane hitting the islands during your week is not the base case, but the season is real, and the surf and wind from a passing storm can affect a beach day even when the storm stays offshore.

## El Niño years

The NHC's central Pacific section says "moderate to strong El Nino years are correlated with increased tropical cyclone activity in the Central Pacific and the occurrence of late season storms." The Climate Prediction Center says the same about strong El Niño conditions.

That link drove NOAA's 2026 outlook, which gave a **70% chance of an above-normal season**, a 20% chance of a near-normal one and a 10% chance of a below-normal one, with **5 to 13 tropical cyclones** across the central Pacific. NOAA's main reason was an expected moderate or stronger El Niño. The outlook also says it "does not indicate where or when any storms may affect land." It is a forecast of basin activity, not of landfall. We have not found an official end-of-season count for 2026, and the season is still open until 30 November. We do not print one.

## Hurricane season is the dry season

Hurricane season and rainy season are not the same thing in Hawaii. The NWS says most of Hawaii has only two seasons: **"summer," between May and October, and "winter," between October and April**. It adds that "Hawaii's heaviest rains come from winter storms between October and April." The central Pacific hurricane season sits almost entirely inside the dry half.

At Honolulu the wettest month is March and the driest is June. At Kahului the wettest is December and the driest is June. Both airports are on the dry, leeward side of their islands. The NWS page warns that rain in Hawaii varies sharply over short distances: amounts are greatest over upper slopes and crests and least in the leeward lowlands. So read this table as the dry-side story. If you are staying on a windward coast, expect more rain than these figures.

## Temperature through the season

The NWS says Hawaii's warmest months are **August and September**, not June and July, and its coolest are **February and March**, because the ocean lags behind the sun. It puts the sea-surface temperature near Hawaii at **73 or 74 °F between late February and March** and **near 80 °F in late September or early October**.

Our tables agree on the warm end. Honolulu's highest average high is 31.5 °C in August, and Kahului's is 32.2 °C in both August and September. On the cool end the stations differ slightly from the statewide rule. At Honolulu January has the lowest average high (26.9 °C) and the lowest average low (19.3 °C). At Kahului the lowest average high, 27.9 °C, is shared by January and February, and January has the lowest low, 17.6 °C.

So the hurricane peak lines up with the warmest air and the warmest water of the year. That is part of why July to September draws visitors in the first place.

## The eastern Pacific: Cabo and Puerto Vallarta

If you are choosing between Hawaii and Mexico's Pacific coast, note that the two run on different clocks. The eastern Pacific season, which covers [Cabo San Lucas](/mexico/cabo-san-lucas/) and [Puerto Vallarta](/mexico/puerto-vallarta/), opens on **15 May**, two weeks before the central Pacific. The National Hurricane Center's 1991–2020 average there is 15 named storms, 8 hurricanes and 4 major hurricanes, a far busier basin than Hawaii's. Both seasons close on 30 November. The Atlantic side, including [Puerto Rico](/puerto-rico/), runs from 1 June to 30 November and peaks on 10 September; the [Puerto Rico sheet](/puerto-rico/#best-time) now has the month-by-month view.

## So when should you go?

This is our editorial view, built only from the figures above.

- **For the lowest hurricane exposure,** go between **December and May**. That is fully outside the central Pacific season. You trade it for the wetter winter half of the year.
- **For the driest weather at the leeward airports,** look at summer. April to September are Honolulu's six driest months, and May to October are Kahului's. Most of that overlaps hurricane season, including the July–September peak.
- **For a compromise,** **April and May** sit outside the hurricane season, after the winter rains have dropped. Honolulu averages 19.5 mm in April and 20.8 mm in May; Kahului averages 33.5 mm and 18.0 mm.

Our month lists use one fixed rule: an average high of 20–30 °C and under 100 mm of rain in the sheet's own table. On that rule, Honolulu and the Hawaii sheet qualify in January through May, November and December. Maui qualifies in January through April, November and December. You can see them next to other places in [January](/best-places-to-visit-in-january/), [April](/best-places-to-visit-in-april/), [May](/best-places-to-visit-in-may/), [November](/best-places-to-visit-in-november/) and [December](/best-places-to-visit-in-december/). From June to October (and in May at Kahului) the Hawaii stations drop off those lists because their average highs pass 30 °C, not because of rain.

## If you are travelling in season

We do not give storm-safety advice of our own. The official sources for the islands are the National Weather Service office in Honolulu and the Central Pacific Hurricane Center, which NOAA's pages list at the same Honolulu address. Check them before and during a trip between June and November. A seasonal outlook will not help here: NOAA says it "is not a landfall forecast."
